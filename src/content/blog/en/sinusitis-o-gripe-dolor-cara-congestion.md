You bend down to tie your shoes and your forehead and cheekbones feel heavy, as if they were full of water. Your nose has been blocked for days, the mucus came out yellow this morning, and a coworker has already told you "that's a sinus infection, you need antibiotics." Before reaching for a pill, it helps to understand what is going on and how it differs from the flu, because the answer changes what will actually make you feel better.

## What the sinuses are and why they hurt

Behind your cheekbones, between your eyes and inside your forehead are air-filled spaces called the sinuses. They normally drain mucus into the nose without you noticing. When a cold or flu virus inflames the inside of the nose, or when allergies keep it irritated, the narrow drainage channels close up. Mucus gets trapped, pressure builds, and you feel that dull ache in your face that gets worse when you lean forward.

That is sinusitis: inflammation of those spaces. According to the CDC, **most sinus infections are caused by viruses and get better on their own**, just like the cold that started them. Only a small share turn into a bacterial infection.

## Clues that set one apart from the other

The flu and a sinus infection can show up together (the flu inflames the nose, then the sinuses block), but each one leaves its own fingerprint. This table sums up what the clinic's medical team listens for when you describe your symptoms:

| Clue | Flu (influenza) | Sinus infection |
|---|---|---|
| How it starts | Suddenly, within a few hours | Gradually, usually after a cold or the flu |
| Fever | Common, sometimes high from day one | Mild or none; a fever that lasts beyond 3 or 4 days is a reason to get checked |
| Pain | All over the body: back, legs, head | Centered in the face: forehead, cheeks, around the eyes, sometimes the upper teeth |
| Fatigue | Severe, hard to get out of bed | Moderate, more of a heavy-headed feeling |
| Nose | May be stuffy, but it is not the main issue | The heart of the problem: congestion, thick mucus and drip down the throat |
| Other complaints | Dry cough, chills, scratchy throat | Bad breath, reduced sense of smell, cough that worsens when lying down |

One thing that confuses many families around Lazybrook and Timbergrove: **mucus color alone does not mean bacteria are involved**. During a viral cold, mucus can turn yellow or green for a few days and then clear up without antibiotics.

## The congestion clock: what is normal and what is not

Rather than any single symptom, what really guides the decision is how your congestion changes over time:

1. **The first few days:** stuffy nose, sneezing, and maybe fever and body aches if it is the flu. This is the stage when a rapid flu and COVID test is worth it, because flu antivirals work best when started in the first two days of symptoms.
2. **Day four to day ten:** facial pressure and thicker mucus are common while your body clears the infection. You should still feel a little better each day, even if only slightly.
3. **More than 10 days with no improvement:** at this point the illness no longer follows the usual viral pattern, and an evaluation makes sense.
4. **You got better, then suddenly worse:** if the fever comes back or the facial pressure returns stronger after a few good days, that is another signal to come in.

The CDC also advises getting care for a severe headache or facial pain, a fever lasting more than 3 or 4 days, or several sinus infections in the past year.

## Why antibiotics are not the first step

Antibiotics do nothing against viruses. With a viral sinus infection they will not speed up recovery, and they can cause diarrhea, an upset stomach or allergic reactions, while also helping resistant bacteria spread. That is why the CDC notes that even when a bacterial sinus infection is suspected, **watchful waiting** for a few days is sometimes recommended before starting them.

At the clinic, an antibiotic is prescribed **only if the clinic's medical team finds signs of a bacterial infection** after examining you and hearing how your symptoms have progressed. If the cause turns out to be the flu and an antiviral is appropriate, that option is considered instead. And if neither is needed, you leave with a clear plan to ease the pressure and know when to come back.

## What eases the pressure while you heal

These general measures help many adults with congestion and sinus pain:

- **Saline nasal rinses:** use distilled, sterile, or boiled-and-cooled water, never water straight from the tap.
- **Steam:** a hot shower or breathing steam from a bowl of warm water loosens mucus.
- **Warm compresses** over your cheeks and forehead for a few minutes.
- **Plenty of fluids** to keep mucus thinner.
- **Sleeping with your head slightly raised** so the sinuses drain better at night.
- **Over-the-counter pain relievers** for pain or fever, following the label.
- **Decongestant nasal sprays** only for the number of days on the package: using them longer can leave your nose even more blocked. If you have high blood pressure, ask before taking decongestant pills.

If you also have confirmed flu, rest at home and do not go back to work until you have gone a full day without fever without using fever-reducing medicine.

## When congestion comes back every season

Some people in Houston bounce from one "sinus infection" to the next every time the weather shifts. Often the root cause is **allergic rhinitis**: pollen, mold and humidity keep the nose inflamed, the sinuses cannot drain well, and the pressure keeps coming back. If you notice sneezing fits, itchy eyes or throat, and congestion that shows up at certain times of year, an [allergy visit](/en/services/alergias) helps pinpoint the trigger and manage congestion with nasal steroids and non-drowsy antihistamines.

Preventing what usually starts the problem also helps: the [flu shot](/en/services/vacunas) lowers the chance that a bout of influenza becomes the opening act for days of blocked sinuses.

## When sinus or flu symptoms mean the ER (or 911)

Do not wait for a clinic visit if any of these show up along with a sinus infection or the flu:

- Swelling or redness around one eye, double vision or other changes in your sight.
- A very severe headache that will not let up, a stiff neck or confusion.
- Swelling over the forehead.
- Trouble breathing, chest pain or pressure, or bluish or purple lips.
