Most people know the moment. By day two or three the burning is gone, the constant trips to the bathroom have stopped, and the rest of the pill pack starts to look optional. That is exactly when many of us tuck the leftovers away "just in case" or hand them to a relative who has similar symptoms. Here is why that reasonable-sounding move tends to backfire, and what to do when you genuinely run into trouble with your treatment.

## Feeling better is not the same as being cured

The discomfort of a bladder infection comes from the irritation bacteria cause in the lining of the bladder. Once the antibiotic kicks in, it wipes out most of those bacteria and the irritation settles quickly. The ones still standing after the first few days, though, tend to be the toughest of the bunch. Stop early and you hand those survivors the room they need to grow back.

That is why the clinic's medical team sets the length of your course around your situation: where the infection is, whether you are pregnant, whether you live with diabetes, whether you are a man, and whether this is a repeat infection. Some courses are short and some are longer, and none of them is picked at random. The practical rule is simple: **take it exactly as directed, down to the last dose**, even if you feel completely fine on day two. If you ever think you should stop, ask the clinic first instead of deciding at the kitchen table.

## What happens when a course is cut short

Quitting early leads to two outcomes we see often:

- **Relapse.** A week or two later the burning is back, sometimes worse than before. It often feels like a brand-new infection when it is really the old one that never fully cleared.
- **Bacteria that are harder to treat.** Every incomplete course gives bacteria a chance to adapt. The CDC describes antibiotic resistance as a serious public health threat: an infection that once cleared with a simple option may end up needing stronger drugs and extra visits.

There is a third, less familiar risk. A bladder infection that is not fully treated can travel up to the kidneys, and then the problem is no longer burning but fever, chills and pain in one side of your back.

## A quick reference for your treatment days

These are the questions that come up most while someone is on a UTI antibiotic:

| What is happening | What to do |
|---|---|
| Symptoms are gone but pills are left | Keep going until the end; improvement means it is working, not that it is done |
| You missed a dose | Do not double up to catch up; call the clinic or ask at the pharmacy how to continue |
| Nausea or an upset stomach | Ask whether you can take it with food; do not stop on your own |
| Hives, an itchy rash or swelling | Stop taking it and contact us right away; if your lips or tongue swell or breathing gets hard, call 911 |
| Very watery, frequent or bloody diarrhea | Let the clinic know soon, even if you are almost finished |
| Vaginal itching or discharge after the antibiotic | It may be a yeast infection; it is checked during a visit |
| No improvement after two or three days on treatment | Come back: the treatment may need a second look |

If remembering doses is the hard part, set phone alarms for the same times every day or keep the box next to your toothbrush. Small habits like these prevent a lot of relapses.

## If the medicine disagrees with you, call before switching

Some stomach upset on an antibiotic is common. What does not help is fixing it by quitting, or by swapping in a different antibiotic someone had at home. Each antibiotic works against certain bacteria and not others, and some are not safe during pregnancy or alongside medications you already take. If the one you were given does not sit well, the clinic's medical team can look at whether another option suits you.

When a **urine culture** is sent out, the result takes a few days. If it shows the bacteria will not respond well to the treatment you started, the clinic reaches out so it can be adjusted. That is one more reason to leave a phone number at the front desk that actually reaches you, and to pick up when we call.

## Do not keep leftovers or share them

If you finish the course as directed, nothing should be left over. And if a few pills are left for some reason, they are not a "first-aid kit for next time." Here is why:

- **The next episode might not be a UTI at all.** Burning when you pee also shows up with irritation, vaginal infections and some sexually transmitted infections, and the clinic's medical team evaluates those differently.
- **A handful of pills is not enough to cure anything**, but it is plenty to teach bacteria how to survive.
- **What worked for your husband, your mom or your neighbor was not chosen for you.** Age, kidney function, allergies and other medications all change the choice.
- **Taking an antibiotic before a urine test can mask the result** and make it harder to tell what is really going on.

To get rid of medicines you no longer need, ask at a pharmacy or look for a drug take-back location near you, and avoid flushing them.

## When to come back

Schedule another visit if:

- You notice no improvement at all after two or three days on treatment.
- You finished the antibiotic and the symptoms returned within a few weeks.
- This is your second or third infection in a few months; it is worth looking for the cause instead of just putting out each fire.
- You have diabetes and your sugar ran high during the infection; [chronic condition](/en/services/condiciones-cronicas) follow-up happens here too.
- New gynecological symptoms show up, such as discharge or itching; our [gynecology](/en/services/ginecologia) visit covers them.

## Signs that cannot wait: ER or 911

Go to an emergency room or call 911 if, during or after treatment, you have a high fever with chills and flank pain, vomiting that keeps you from holding down water or your pills, severe confusion (especially in older adults), you cannot pee at all, you faint, or your face, lips or tongue swell and breathing becomes difficult.
